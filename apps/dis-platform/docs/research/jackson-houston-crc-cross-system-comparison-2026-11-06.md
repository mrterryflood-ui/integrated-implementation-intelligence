# DIS applied research: Similar late-stage colorectal cancer proportions, dissimilar systems
**Evidence snapshot:** 2026-11-06 · **Status:** Public-source analytical case, not a completed trial, live RPLICE run, formal diagnosis, institutional engagement or causal estimate.
**Question:** Why do Hinds County, Mississippi (Jackson proxy) and Harris County, Texas (Houston proxy) have nearly identical fractions of diagnosed colorectal cancers that are regional or distant stage when their contexts and incidence burdens differ? Is that commonality statewide, and which system mechanisms should be tested?

## ORIENT: distinct metrics and scope
NCI State Cancer Profiles, diagnosis period 2018–2022, all ages, sexes and races. **Late-stage share** = late-stage incident diagnoses / all incident colorectal diagnoses (not fraction of all residents). **Late-stage incidence** = age-adjusted annual cases per 100,000 residents (not the late-stage share). The numerator/denominator and age adjustment differ. Regional+distant is not synonymous with distant-only Stage IV. The residence county is a geographic proxy, not a city-level estimate.

| Area | Percent of diagnosed CRC cases late-stage | Late-stage age-adjusted incidence /100k | Average annual late-stage cases |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Hinds County, MS | 63.3 | 30.4 | 76 |
| Harris County, TX | 63.4 | 23.3 | 1,044 |
| Mississippi | 62.4 | 28.9 | 1,025 |
| Texas | 61.0 | 23.3 | 6,995 |
| United States | 59.9 | 22.0 | 86,932 |

**Interpretation:** Similar stage share cannot demonstrate similar risk, biology, timeliness, affordability or care systems. Mississippi's higher *late-stage incidence* needs its own explanation.

**County contrasts with more stable denominators:**
- Mississippi: Rankin 61.0% / 21.1; Madison 55.9% / 20.0; Lauderdale 51.6% / 23.8; Jackson County (Gulf Coast, NOT Jackson city) 68.3% / 31.3; Lowndes 70.2% / 29.7.
- Texas: Fort Bend 58.1% / 19.7; Montgomery 65.5% / 22.7; Liberty 69.2% / 35.8; Dallas 62.5% / 22.9; Bexar 59.3% / 22.2; Travis 63.7% / 21.8; El Paso 55.6% / 20.5; Denton 59.0% / 18.1.
- Small-count extremes such as Tippah MS 80.3% (11 annual late-stage cases) and Red River TX 77.1% (5 cases) need uncertainty and suppression analyses before rank/causal conclusions.

**Newer and different cohort:** The NCI national-by-state 2019–2023 table lists stage shares Mississippi 63.0%, Texas 61.5%, U.S. 60.2% and incidence rates 28.9/23.7/22.3, respectively. This is a partially overlapping interval, NOT an independent before/after program evaluation. The county comparison above is 2018–2022; do not mix periods in pooled statistical analysis.

## AUDIT and MAP: what is comparable, what is not

**Population-level screening, BRFSS via Commonwealth Fund (2024), adults ages 45–74:** MS 66.8%, TX 66.5%, US 70.5%. Survey-based, different cohort and period from cancer-stage data.
**Health center colorectal screening quality measure, HRSA UDS 2024:** Mississippi 37.32%, Texas 33.93%. This is a different setting, denominator and reporting method than BRFSS. Never subtract these percentages as a valid individual-level disparity or assert that they estimate all people in the counties.
**Policy:** Texas and Mississippi had not implemented full ACA Medicaid expansion as of October 2026; a potential common structural mechanism, NOT a proven explanation for the observed cancer-stage numbers.

**Historical eligibility:** In 2021, routine average-risk screening recommendations expanded to age 45. Enlarged eligible denominators can change reported screening prevalence even without a comparable deterioration in previously eligible patients. Standardize guideline age and risk criteria before causal claims.

**Programs reported (not necessarily partner capabilities):**
- Mississippi: 2023 'Focus on 45' awareness and access effort associated with Mississippi Colorectal Cancer Roundtable and UMMC; independent stage-shift outcome unverified here.
- Texas: MD Anderson/CPRIT Project 80%, FIT kits, partner clinics, navigation, bilingual outreach and abnormal-result follow-up. The FY2025 report states 135,020 people screened during 2014–2024, 176 clinics in 60+ Texas counties. The ACS 2025 program interview reports 68.8% colonoscopy completion **among those referred** after abnormal FIT, and a Houston-area clinic system's reported screening increase from 7% in 2017 to >80% in most later years. These are different process denominators and program self-reports, NOT evidence that the statewide late-stage incidence fell because of Project 80%.
- No local clinic, state authority, investigator, beneficiary or institution has agreed to join this DIS case.

## GAP: open-world competing explanations and falsifiers

| Hypothesis | Plausible mechanism | Evidence that discriminates it | Counterexplanation / concern |
|---|---|---|---|
| H1: Underlying cancer risk varies more than stage fraction | Age, genetics, biology, carcinogenic exposure, obesity, diet, and other exposures affect incidence | All-stage incidence by age, sex, race, geography; risk profiles; stage fractions | Confounding, ecological bias, variable detection intensity |
| H2: Shared screening access barriers | Insurance, limited primary care, cost, transport, mistrust, language, scheduling | Eligible cohort offered/overdue/completed screening; claims/EHR + lived experience | Differences in screening eligibility, test modality and recall |
| H3: Post-positive-FIT diagnostic pathway is the key bottleneck | Lost contact, missed referral, insufficient colonoscopy capacity, poor navigation | Dated abnormal FIT → notice → referral → scheduled → performed → pathologic resolution | External care unrecorded, case complexity, clinical appropriateness |
| H4: Delayed evaluation of symptoms or interval cancers | Symptoms not acted upon; cancers despite screening, differential aggressiveness | Audit symptoms, family-risk routes, test intervals and tumor biology | Not all advanced cancers are preventable by more screening alone |
| H5: Shared policy but different operational systems | Nonexpansion, payer fragmentation, navigators and safety-net capacity | Stratified service-path and insurance analysis with institutional data | Policy alone cannot explain county-to-county heterogeneity |
| H6: Measurement/case-mix effect | Case ascertainment, stage unknownness, denominator changes, 2021 age expansion | Registry coding, missing stage, sensitivity to cohort definition, uncertainty intervals | True disease and access differences remain possible |

**Required research discipline:** Do not infer that lifestyle is irrelevant from two matched fractions. Identify shared mechanisms and divergent causes separately. Similar-looking proportions can arise through very different causal pathways. Do not treat a case-share metric as risk in the general population.

## Evidence on possible delivery mechanisms

A 2026 safety-net retrospective study reported only 36% of 728 patients with abnormal FIT completed a follow-up colonoscopy within six months; substantial attrition occurred at referral and scheduling. This is **not** a study of Houston or Jackson; transfer requires testing.
A 2024 pragmatic FQHC RCT found six-month CRC screening completion of 30.0% with mailed FIT plus as-needed navigation compared with 9.7% under usual care among 4,002 randomized patients. This shows an actionable intervention mechanism, not a confirmed stage-shift effect in Mississippi or Texas.
A 2026 follow-up trial analysis found 69.0% vs 38.7% one-year colonoscopy completion with centralized navigation versus usual care among 89 FIT-positive participants; the subgroup is small and was studied in North Carolina, not these sites.
A 2026 JAMA report on 20,250 FQHC patients found mailed FIT outreach's early screening benefit was not sustained at three years, highlighting the **day-after-success/sustainment** gap.

## ALIGN: HIL method choice and chain web
DIS coordinates evidence, context, measurement, decisions, translation and learning. Human decision-makers select RPLICE-owned specialist methods through the existing MCP boundary: SALP to examine structural anchors and leverage; Parallax for **operator-declared, not verified-real-person** perspectives; implementation frameworks (e.g., CFIR) for contextual determinants; fidelity and adaptation reviews where suitable. Selecting SALP should not trigger a blanket methodological HOLD. Method selection does not confer access to protected patient data, enact a clinical intervention or signify that the remote RPLICE tool has run.

**Designed reality:** USPSTF-appropriate screening, prompt follow-up, definitive diagnosis and care.
**Operational reality to audit:** Clinics, health centers, insurers and referral systems differ in the capacity to complete the path.
**Experienced reality to elicit:** Whether patients get the offer, can afford/complete testing, understand results, reach specialists, and experience appropriate care in time.

**Chain nodes:** insurance/eligibility → primary care EHR/recall → screening offer and test delivery → abnormal result → communications → referral → appointment → colonoscopy capacity → pathology → staging → treatment → long-term follow-up. Connect employment/leave, transportation, disability access, housing, language, community confidence, state/federal policy, workforce, and family history; label indirect pathways, uncertain edges and provenance.

## ACT/TRACK/ADAPT: proposed evaluation, not executed

1. Obtain registry-level, period-matched county/stage data and confidence intervals across all available MS and TX counties; report suppression, small-area uncertainty and age-adjusted rates separately from stage shares.
2. Stratify stage and screening by age, sex, race/ethnicity, insurance, rurality, clinic type and risk status when sample size and permitted data allow. Separate 45–49 from previously eligible groups; inspect early-onset symptomatic cases.
3. Obtain authorization for de-identified site-level care cascade: eligible → offered → completed → abnormal → referred → scheduled → diagnostic colonoscopy at 3/6/12 months → pathology → disposition; audit missing out-of-network follow-up and patient experiences.
4. Use comparative and spatial models for association; define a prospective site-level intervention and comparator with a preregistered analysis plan. If choosing triple-difference (DDD), compare pilot vs matched nonpilot sites, pre/post intervention, and a prespecified third dimension (e.g., insurance or safety-net status), conditional on comparable coding, measurement, parallel-trend diagnostics and enough clusters. Do NOT apply DDD to two cross-sectional percentages.
5. Candidate intervention: reliable closed-loop follow-up for abnormal FIT, integrating patient-chosen navigation, scheduling access, travel and prep support, and EHR referral accountability. Select specific components with local stakeholders, capacity assessment and HIL method review; evaluate against usual care with equity and burden safeguards.
6. **SMART process measures pending baseline/resource approval:** eligible screening completion, abnormal-FIT-to-colonoscopy completion within six months, median days to resolution, share lost to follow-up, stage incidence and subgroup differences; track total navigator/clinic workload and costs. Set credible effect targets after actual baseline power/capacity estimates. An initial 12-month pilot can assess diagnostic process, but not claim a population cancer-stage reduction from short follow-up.
7. Temporal stewardship: retrospective 10-year timeline; current measurement state; day-after-success handoffs and staffing; long-term monitoring, drift and transfer rules. Evaluate whether Texas solutions transfer to Mississippi only after mechanism and local-context validation.
